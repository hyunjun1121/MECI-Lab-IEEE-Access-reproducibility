\begin{abstract}
This paper evaluates whether a time-indexed content representation can organize short-form video segments for editorial review alongside owner-authorized YouTube Analytics. The primary study uses a frozen public Shorts-tab frame from an official military channel: 197 catalog items, 194 strict non-fallback outputs from the TRImodal Brain Encoder version 2 (TRIBE v2), 190 videos with non-empty retention reports, and 1,745 five-second analysis windows. \rev{All cortical quantities are model-derived predictions used as content descriptors, not measurements of viewer brain activity.} TRIBE v2 predicted cortical values were summarized into six anatomically defined cortical profiles and whole-cortex change descriptors, then aligned to the platform time axis. After cubic position adjustment\rev{ in five-second windows}, cingulo-parietal and anterior-insular profiles showed the largest associations with audience watch ratio\rev{, although in a post hoc spatial-control comparison no profile exceeded rotated cortical regions of the same shape after multiple-comparison correction}, while cortical pattern change was associated with segment-level exit intensity. In Ridge evaluation of five-second windows grouped by video, adding profiles and temporal changes reduced exit-intensity mean absolute error from 0.006237 to 0.005954; the chronological point estimate changed from 0.006476 to 0.006090, with an interval that included zero. Improvements were not uniform across outcomes\rev{ or model families}. A separate 2,312-video public-view channel analysis did not support cross-channel transfer. \rev{All reported associations and prediction gains are therefore specific to the primary channel.} The contribution is an auditable workflow for organizing time-local editorial review, not a measurement of viewer brain activity, psychological state, learning, or causality.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\PARstart{S}{hort-form} video is treated here as a format for public communication, education, and institutional storytelling. Research on short-video platforms describes an established communication and cultural format \cite{kaye2022tiktok}, and studies of YouTube Shorts document measurable interactions between short-form distribution and audience behavior \cite{rajendran2024shorts}. Public communication campaigns are a long-standing instrument of institutional outreach \cite{rice2013campaigns}, and military advertising has also been empirically evaluated as an institutional communication activity \cite{dertouzos2003military}. In online educational video, production choices and viewing behavior can be measured at scale \cite{guo2014video,breslow2013studying}, while multimedia-learning theory describes how audiovisual design shapes the presentation and processing of information \cite{mayer2009multimedia}. These findings motivate a content-analysis problem: can a video be represented in a time-local form that makes editorial questions measurable?

Audience-retention reports preserve a video-time axis. The YouTube Analytics application programming interface (API) provides aggregate audience-watch-ratio and relative-retention measures indexed by elapsed video position, including reports for specific owner-authorized content \cite{youtubeRetentionAPI}. These are platform observations; they do not by themselves identify why an individual stopped watching, whether the content was understood, or whether learning occurred \cite{gasevic2015learning}. A second representation is needed if the analysis is to describe the content itself rather than only its final platform outcome.

The TRImodal Brain Encoder version 2 (TRIBE v2) is one possible representation. It combines visual, auditory, and language inputs to predict values on a cortical surface from naturalistic audiovisual stimuli \cite{dascoli2026tribev2}, extending an earlier tri-modal whole-brain encoder \cite{dascoli2025tribe}. In this study, the prediction is used as a structured, time-varying descriptor of the audiovisual stimulus. It is not treated as measured functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), a neural response from a Korean viewer, or evidence that a named psychological state occurred.

The contribution of this paper is a documented, auditable evaluation procedure rather than a new brain model. The procedure joins a fixed multimodal representation to owner-authorized platform observations, preserves their common video-time axis, and tests whether the representation adds information beyond basic video metadata. It is evaluated on a complete frozen Shorts-tab frame from an official military channel and stress-tested with a separate public-view channel. The study addresses three questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item After adjusting for the common position of a segment within a video, are TRIBE-derived content descriptors associated with audience retention and viewing termination?
    \item Under the same video-grouped evaluation split, does adding region-based cortical descriptors and temporal pattern changes improve prediction for five-second windows relative to basic metadata?
    \item Can the combined representation nominate concrete video segments for human editorial review without becoming an automated decision rule?
\end{enumerate}

The paper makes four contributions. First, it documents an explicit alignment procedure for persisted model segments and owner-authorized retention reports. Second, it separates video-level summaries from within-video temporal analysis. Third, it compares metadata-only and representation-enriched models for five-second windows while keeping all windows from a video in the same evaluation fold, including a same-family nonlinear audit. Fourth, it reports a negative cross-channel transfer result as a practical boundary \rev{that confines the workflow's present evidence to the primary channel}. The central claim is procedural: model-derived cortical representations can be evaluated as one content-analysis input for temporal editorial review, but their usefulness is outcome-specific and requires prospective validation.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Audience behavior as an aggregate signal}
Audience-retention curves make an otherwise diffuse viewing process observable along a video-time axis, with platform-defined measures for elapsed position and audience behavior \cite{youtubeRetentionAPI}. Large-scale course and lecture-video studies show that engagement varies systematically with in-video position and that dropout and rewatching concentrate at identifiable timestamps \cite{breslow2013studying,kim2014dropouts}. Short-form platform research documents the reach and production culture of the format \cite{kaye2022tiktok} and its measurable interaction with long-form viewing \cite{rajendran2024shorts}. These curves can reveal broad position effects and local changes, but they remain aggregate platform statistics. They do not identify the reason for an individual departure, comprehension, or learning. This distinction is consistent with cautions in learning analytics that behavioral traces should be interpreted in relation to a substantive learning question rather than treated as self-sufficient evidence \cite{gasevic2015learning}. Here, retention is an external aggregate outcome for evaluating a content representation.

\subsection{Multimodal video representation}
Contemporary video understanding rests on learned multimodal and self-supervised representations: image--language contrastive models transfer natural-language supervision to vision \cite{radford2021clip}, multi-rate architectures capture temporal structure at coarse and fine resolutions \cite{feichtenhofer2019slowfast}, and latent-prediction models learn video representations without labels \cite{assran2025vjepa2}; survey treatments formalize how such synchronized streams are combined \cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}. The present representation retains synchronized visual, auditory, and language streams because TRIBE v2 is designed as a tri-modal model for naturalistic stimuli \cite{dascoli2026tribev2}. Evidence from online educational videos links production choices with observed viewing behavior \cite{guo2014video}; however, an aggregate viewing outcome does not by itself provide a time-local explanation of a particular channel's content. The present study retains the original multimodal model output and evaluates how it behaves when aligned with an independently collected platform report.

\subsection{Brain-predictive representations}
TRIBE v2 is a foundation model for predicting cortical responses to naturalistic audiovisual stimuli \cite{dascoli2026tribev2}, extending an earlier tri-modal whole-brain encoder \cite{dascoli2025tribe}. The underlying research program is well established: encoding models formally predict measured brain responses from stimulus features \cite{naselaris2011encoding}; natural-image identification \cite{kay2008identifying}, natural-movie reconstruction \cite{nishimoto2011naturalmovies}, and semantic maps derived from natural speech \cite{huth2016naturalspeech} related time-varying naturalistic stimuli to distributed cortical representations; and feature hierarchies optimized for vision proved predictive of cortical response patterns, including dynamic natural vision \cite{yamins2014performance,wen2018neural}. Parallel efforts align pretrained multimodal features to movie-evoked responses \cite{villanueva2025predicting}. Those studies motivate a structured stimulus representation; they do not validate the present channel application. A cortical-surface prediction is not equivalent to a measured response in a new audience. Its value here is narrower and testable: it supplies a structured, high-dimensional representation of stimulus content that can be summarized over time. An anatomical cortical atlas defines the parcel masks used for six region-based summaries; it provides spatial boundaries, not psychological labels or evidence that a viewer state occurred \cite{destrieux2010parcellation}. \rev{The six summarized territories lie in or near cortical systems that the functional literature associates with processing relevant to audiovisual content: subjective valuation in ventromedial prefrontal cortex \mbox{\cite{bartra2013valuation}}; salience processing in anterior insular cortex \mbox{\cite{seeley2007salience}}; mental-state attribution at the temporo-parietal junction \mbox{\cite{saxe2003people}}; the default-mode system, including posterior cingulate cortex \mbox{\cite{raichle2001default}}; and early visual \mbox{\cite{wandell2007visual}} and auditory \mbox{\cite{griffiths2002planum}} cortex. \mbox{Section~IV-A} describes this correspondence, its anatomical limits, and its role in the analysis.}

\subsection{Prediction and explanation}
A model that improves a held-out error metric is not thereby a causal explanation of audience behavior. Prediction and explanation answer different questions and require different designs \cite{yarkoni2017prediction,shmueli2010explain}. Comparisons between matched training and test distributions further show that apparent generalization can disappear under distribution shift \cite{recht2019imagenet}. Accordingly, this paper reports error, robustness, and transfer behavior as evaluation results. It does not infer that a cortical descriptor causes retention, that a scene transition caused an audience change, or that the model can replace human editorial judgment.

\section{Data and Study Design}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Primary channel and frozen frame}
The primary frame was a frozen public Shorts-tab catalog for YugsaTV, an official military channel. The catalog contained 197 public items at the snapshot date. The channel owner authorized read-only access to YouTube Analytics for these reports. The analysis cutoff was September 12, 2026, and the Analytics reporting timezone was America/Los\_Angeles. Retention reports were queried from each video's publication date through the common cutoff; observation windows therefore differ across videos.

The unit of analysis is the video record and its time-indexed aggregate report. No participants were recruited or measured. The analysis contains no individual viewer records, individual service-member records, comment text, or private person-level data. Raw video and audio files are not redistributed. Owner-authorized Analytics responses remain private; only aggregate derived tables and figures needed for the manuscript are staged in the paper package.

\subsection{Nested inclusion frame}
The fixed inference run produced 194 strict, non-fallback TRIBE v2 outputs. Three catalog items were excluded before prediction because the run configuration rejected records whose unmatched-word ratio exceeded 0.05: \texttt{HnyZ72iYlH0} (1/18), \texttt{XiPeSUCdcHM} (1/1), and \texttt{nYGyDessKGY} (1/7). The threshold was fixed in the execution configuration before this result analysis; it was not preregistered. No audio-only, video-only, or simulated fallback was used. Among the 194 predicted videos, 190 returned non-empty retention reports. Four videos returned empty Analytics responses and were retained as missing rather than converted to zero retention. The primary five-second analysis contained 1,745 valid windows. The auxiliary transfer channel is Korean Forces Network (KFN). Table~\ref{tab:coverage} reports the nested frame.

KFN Shorts supplied a separate public-metadata stress test. Its frozen catalog contained 2,321 items and 2,312 strict, non-fallback outputs. \section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Model-derived content representation}
TRIBE v2 processes synchronized visual, auditory, and language inputs \cite{dascoli2026tribev2} and produces predicted values on an fsaverage5 cortical surface with 20,484 vertices in the persisted run. In the persisted configuration, the visual stream uses frozen V-JEPA~2 features \cite{assran2025vjepa2}, the auditory stream uses a frozen W2v-BERT~2.0 checkpoint \cite{chung2021w2vbert}, and the language stream uses frozen Llama~3.2 features \cite{grattafiori2024llama3}; each backbone serves as a fixed feature extractor, consistent with the model's published configuration. The fsaverage5 surface is a standard coarse cortical reference geometry from the FreeSurfer suite \cite{fischl2012freesurfer}. The visual input used 64 frames from a four-second clip, while the model produced one-second prediction intervals. These are different temporal resolutions, not a one-clip-to-one-output mapping: each prediction row is indexed by the explicit start and stop boundaries returned for its segment. The persisted intervals were contiguous and one second long in all 194 strict outputs.

The six profiles are unweighted means of predicted values over fixed parcel masks from an anatomical cortical atlas \cite{destrieux2010parcellation}. \rev{Each profile is named for the cortical territory its parcels cover: frontal, anterior-insular, cingulo-parietal, posterior-cingulate, visual-cortical, and auditory-cortical. The names describe anatomy, not a function measured in viewers. Together the masks form a compact, fixed set of distinct cortical territories for descriptive comparison (\mbox{Table~\ref{tab:profiles}}).}

\rev{\textit{Functional correspondence.} Naturalistic viewing engages cortex well beyond early sensory areas, and responses to the same film are synchronized across viewers \mbox{\cite{hasson2004intersubject}}. The six territories relate to functionally characterized systems as follows. The frontal profile (frontomarginal and rectus gyri) covers ventromedial and orbital prefrontal cortex near the valuation network identified by meta-analysis \mbox{\cite{bartra2013valuation}}. The anterior-insular profile covers the anterior insula, a node of the salience network \mbox{\cite{seeley2007salience}}. The angular-gyrus part of the cingulo-parietal profile lies in the inferior parietal territory of the temporo-parietal junction, which is engaged when people attribute mental states to others \mbox{\cite{saxe2003people}}. The posterior-cingulate profile covers a core region of the default-mode system \mbox{\cite{raichle2001default}}. The visual and auditory profiles cover early visual cortex along the calcarine sulcus \mbox{\cite{wandell2007visual}} and the planum temporale and transverse temporal gyrus \mbox{\cite{griffiths2002planum}}. For video content, these correspondences yield hypotheses rather than established mappings. Valuation- and salience-related territories may vary with appraisal-relevant or salient events, temporo-parietal and default-mode territories with social and narrative content, and sensory territories with visual and acoustic structure.}

\rev{\textit{Anatomical consistency.} The cited functional studies report peaks in volume coordinates, whereas the profiles are surface parcels. We therefore mapped the reported peaks onto the cortical surface (Appendix~A, \mbox{Table~\ref{tab:crosswalk}}). The correspondence is partial. The left frontoinsular peak fell inside the anterior-insular profile. The right frontoinsular and right temporo-parietal peaks lay outside their profiles, within about 5~mm. The ventromedial prefrontal peak lay 8.4~mm from the frontal profile. The left temporo-parietal and posterior-cingulate peaks were uncertain because surface and volume labels disagreed, and the latter lay 11.9~mm from its profile. The visual and auditory profiles correspond to their literature by anatomical definition. The anterior-mid cingulate part of the cingulo-parietal profile has no direct support in the cited studies, so its two components are not assumed to share a single function. These studies are therefore cited as functional context for the territories, not as localization of the specific parcels.}

\rev{\textit{Predicted responses as content representation.} The reason for summarizing predicted rather than measured responses is the encoding model's validation, which is separate from the territory rationale. TRIBE v2 was trained and evaluated on measured fMRI responses to naturalistic stimuli. Its authors report that predictions for multimodal stimuli are significant across most of the cortex. They also report the largest multimodal gains near the temporo-parieto-occipital junction and smaller gains in prefrontal cortex \mbox{\cite{dascoli2026tribev2}}. Summarizing predictions over fixed territories follows region-of-interest practice in encoding analyses \mbox{\cite{naselaris2011encoding}}. The TRIBE v2 report gives no accuracy for these specific parcels, so how well each profile represents content may differ across territories.}

\rev{\textit{Design history.} The six parcel sets were specified in May 2026 for an earlier content-indexing pipeline. They were used unchanged in a channel analysis committed on 23 August 2026. The earliest preserved retention data were collected on 14 September 2026. The functional literature above was systematized in this revision rather than documented at the original specification, and no parcel definition was changed in response to retention results. The correspondences motivate territory coverage. They do not turn the profiles into measurements of valuation, salience, social cognition, or narrative engagement in viewers.}

Legacy feature identifiers are listed only to map these reader-facing names to the released analysis files. For adjacent five-second cortical vectors $\mathbf{x}_{j-1}$ and $\mathbf{x}_j$, pattern change is $1-r_j$, where $r_j$ is their Pearson correlation after centering each vector across vertices. Mean absolute cortical change is $M_j=V^{-1}\sum_{v=1}^{V}|x_{j,v}-x_{j-1,v}|$, with $V=20{,}484$; this is stored as \texttt{amplitude\_change} in the analysis files. Both descriptors are computed from adjacent overlap-weighted five-second aggregates of the persisted one-second outputs; the first comparison and constant-vector pattern correlations are undefined and omitted. They describe changes in the model's content representation, not neural events or physiological transitions. Appendix A defines the profiles and both change measures.

\subsection{Time alignment and outcomes}
\rev{The primary analysis used five-second windows, with three- and ten-second windows considered in sensitivity analyses. Window width sets a trade-off between temporal resolution, the number of adjacent one-second cortical predictions averaged in each window, and the number of windows available within each video. Under the eight-valid-window eligibility rule for the position-adjusted association, the audience-watch-ratio analysis retained 186, 140, and 4 videos at three-, five-, and ten-second widths, respectively; every video eligible at a longer width is also eligible at the shorter ones. Appendix~B reports results across the three widths.} Each saved cortical prediction is paired with the model-returned segment's start and stop times. TRIBE v2 documents a five-second backward offset in its predictions to compensate for hemodynamic lag; we use the returned segment timeline unchanged and do not add a second shift. These one-second intervals and the Analytics one-percent position bins were mapped onto the same elapsed-video axis. Each analysis window was retained only when the cortical intervals covered it and the available Analytics bins covered it; cortical and Analytics values were combined with their respective temporal-overlap weights, and missing bins were not bridged.

Three primary outcomes were used. The first is the platform audience watch-ratio report, which can exceed one when repeat watching is included. The second is the platform's relative-retention report, interpreted according to its comparison definition. These platform definitions and the available elapsed-position fields are documented by YouTube \cite{youtubeRetentionAPI}. The third, exit intensity, was calculated for each aligned window as \(\texttt{stoppedWatching}/\texttt{totalSegmentImpressions}\). The two count fields were fractionally allocated from the one-percent Analytics bins by temporal overlap; if any required bin was missing, the window outcome was missing, and a zero or nonfinite denominator produced a missing value. Exit intensity is an aggregate segment statistic, not an individual hazard rate or a probability that a particular viewer exited. The exact platform field names are retained in the machine-readable audit tables.

\subsection{Position adjustment and association}
Retention can vary systematically with video position. In-video dropout studies report position-dependent audience behavior \cite{kim2014dropouts}, and multimedia-learning research treats segmentation of material as a first-order design factor \cite{mayer2009multimedia}. For each video, normalized elapsed position $p\in[0,1]$ was entered as a cubic polynomial, $\beta_0+\beta_1p+\beta_2p^2+\beta_3p^3$, and this fitted trend was removed separately from the feature and outcome series \cite{hastie2009esl}. The cubic form allows a smooth curved position trend rather than assuming a linear one. Residuals were compared using a within-video Spearman rank correlation \cite{spearman1904association}. A video-feature-outcome combination was retained only with at least eight valid paired windows, leaving at least four residual degrees of freedom after estimating the four polynomial coefficients. The cubic order and eight-window rule were not preregistered\rev{; no record documents why these values were chosen or shows that alternatives were compared (Section~VI)}. The reported estimate is the arithmetic mean of video-level correlations. Intervals use complete-video bootstrap resampling with 2,000 repetitions and seed 33 \cite{efron1979bootstrap}.

The submission audit applies the same cubic residualization before 500 circular-shift null correlations per eligible video-feature-outcome combination, in the spirit of surrogate-data testing for time series \cite{theiler1992surrogate}. It also constructs a matched transition for each high pattern-change event using the nearest non-event transition within the same video by normalized position. These checks are robustness diagnostics, not significance tests.

\subsection{Window-level prediction with video-grouped evaluation}
The prediction observation is a five-second window; video identity is the grouping unit for evaluation. The primary comparison uses video-grouped Ridge models \cite{hoerl1970ridge}. The metadata-only model contains duration, title length, description length, publication day, normalized position, and a deterministic topic label. This label is assigned by a fixed weighted keyword-rule classifier applied to each video's public title, description, and tags. Supported labels come from eight predefined content categories; records receiving the classifier's weak-fallback status are coded as the separate \texttt{unclassified} level. This is a metadata baseline, not a label generated by TRIBE v2. The two enriched feature sets add the six profile means and then the whole-cortex change descriptors. Standardization, imputation, categorical encoding, and Ridge alpha selection over $[0.01,0.1,1,10,100,1000]$ occur within the training fold; this separation matters because using the same data for model selection and error estimation can bias reported performance \cite{varma2006crossvalidation}. All windows from one video remain in one fold. A chronological 80/20 split provides a second time-ordered check. Mean absolute error (MAE) improvement is defined as metadata-only MAE minus enriched-model MAE, so positive values indicate lower error for the enriched model. \rev{MAE and $R^2$ weight each window by the inverse of the number of windows in its video, so that each video contributes equally (video-equal weighting); the pooled Spearman correlation weights all windows equally. The paired MAE improvement is the mean over videos of the per-video difference, with a 95\% interval from 2,000 video-bootstrap resamples.}

The audit also fits the same fixed HistGradientBoosting implementation from scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}, a histogram-based gradient boosting design in the LightGBM family \cite{ke2017lightgbm}, to metadata-only and enriched feature sets. This prevents an apparent nonlinear improvement from being attributed to TRIBE features when the algorithm has changed at the same time. These nonlinear results are reported in Appendix B; the interpretable Ridge comparison is the primary manuscript result.

\subsection{Cross-channel stress-test features}
For the auxiliary transfer analysis, the target is cumulative public view count transformed as $\log(1+\text{views})$ at the frozen metadata snapshots (August 24, 2026, for YugsaTV and August 23, 2026, for KFN). Both channel models use $\log(1+\text{duration in seconds})$ and $\log(1+\text{upload age in days at the snapshot})$; views are not converted to a per-day rate. The enriched model adds the six duration-weighted cortical profile means. Imputation, scaling, and Ridge regularization selection over $[0.01,0.1,1,10,100,1000]$ are fit using only the source channel, then applied unchanged to the held-out target channel. The test uses public views, not owner-authorized retention measures.

\subsection{Pattern-change review candidates}
Within each video, candidate events were selected from the top decile of the pattern-change descriptor with at least ten seconds between candidates. A candidate required a contiguous preceding, current, and following five-second window, so endpoints and incomplete transitions were excluded. The adjacent before and after windows were compared. The matched control was the nearest non-event transition in the same video by normalized position. The procedure produced 93 candidate transitions across 85 videos and 92 complete event-control pairs across 84 videos; the mean normalized-position distance was 0.1918. The resulting display is a review queue: it identifies locations for a human to inspect, not scenes that an algorithm should automatically remove.

\subsection{Reproducibility and data governance}
The analysis was run from the immutable aggregate-analysis manifest and the verified local TRIBE release. The submission audit records the source hashes, the 0.05 alignment threshold, four-second model input clip, one-second persisted interval, three/five/ten-second window widths, analysis cutoff, timezone, seed, bootstrap count, null permutations, feature sets, and fold rules. The public GitHub repository contains the manuscript source, selected figure-generation code, and publication figures. The Zenodo version ev{1.0.3} record listed in the Data Availability statement contains the accompanying sanitized aggregate dataset: selected result and audit tables, configuration, feature dictionary, and provenance. It contains no analysis scripts, separate checksum-list file, or pre-rendered figures. This release follows the findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable (FAIR) data-stewardship principles \cite{wilkinson2016fair}, but does not reproduce the full analysis from raw inputs, rerun TRIBE v2 inference, or recollect YouTube Analytics reports. Raw media, raw or video/window-level Analytics data, model weights, runtime caches, and private owner records are excluded.

\subsection{Position-adjusted associations}
Figure~\ref{fig:associations} and Table~\ref{tab:associations} summarize the primary associations. The cingulo-parietal profile had the largest mean association with audience watch ratio ($\rho=0.437$, 95\% video-bootstrap interval $[0.361,0.508]$, 140 videos), followed by the anterior-insular profile ($\rho=0.417$, $[0.339,0.490]$). Cortical pattern change had the largest association with exit intensity ($\rho=0.281$, $[0.195,0.366]$, 109 videos); mean absolute cortical change was similar ($\rho=0.278$, $[0.194,0.360]$). For relative retention, the cingulo-parietal profile reached $\rho=0.347$.

The position-adjusted circular-shift audit produced near-zero null means for the same comparisons: the cingulo-parietal and anterior-insular profiles were approximately $-0.011$ and $-0.009$ for audience watch ratio, while cortical pattern change was approximately $-0.026$ for exit intensity. These values are included as a design check rather than converted into a claim of statistical significance.

\rev{\textit{Spatial-control analysis (post hoc, added in this revision).} The circular-shift audit does not ask whether a profile's association exceeds that of other cortical regions. We therefore compared each profile with spatially rotated copies of its own mask \mbox{\cite{alexanderbloch2018spatial}}. Each mask was rotated on the spherical cortical surface, and the same rotation was mirrored across hemispheres. A rotated region was kept only when at least 80\% of its vertices fell outside the medial wall, so the null distribution is conditional on that criterion. For each profile, 10,000 such regions were scored with the same statistic and the same 140-video audience-watch-ratio cohort. All six profiles met the predefined size, area, spread, and connectivity criteria for comparability with their rotated regions. Holm correction was applied across the six profiles. No profile reached a Holm-corrected $p<0.05$ (Appendix~B, \mbox{Table~\ref{tab:spin}}). The cingulo-parietal association exceeded 98.8\% of rotated regions (spin $p=0.012$, Holm $p=0.072$), a nominal result. The anterior-insular association exceeded 93.8\% ($p=0.062$), and the other four profiles exceeded 18--74\%. This comparison therefore did not provide evidence that any profile's association exceeded that of rotated regions after correction. It does not show that the associations lack spatial specificity: with 140 videos and about ten windows per video, its power is limited. Appendix~B reports sensitivity analyses. In two of them, a parcel-level rotation and the relative-retention outcome, the cingulo-parietal profile fell below a family-wise Holm $p$ of 0.05 (0.047 and 0.049). With Holm correction across all 72 reported spin tests (twelve families of six profiles; Appendix~B), however, the smallest corrected value was 0.57, so these sensitivity results are not used as a separate conclusion.}

\subsection{Incremental prediction is outcome-specific}
The same-family Ridge comparison is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:prediction} and Table~\ref{tab:prediction}. Adding region means and temporal changes reduced grouped exit-intensity MAE from 0.006237 to 0.005954, a video-equal improvement of 0.000283 with a 95\% bootstrap interval of [0.000124, 0.000442]. The chronological point estimate changed from 0.006476 to 0.006090, but its interval included zero (improvement 0.000386, 95\% interval [-0.000013, 0.000791]). \rev{For the other two outcomes, the grouped changes had intervals that included zero: audience-watch-ratio MAE increased from 0.092979 to 0.094006 (improvement $-0.001028$, 95\% interval [$-0.003325$, 0.001148]), and relative-retention MAE decreased numerically from 0.076750 to 0.076372 (improvement 0.000378, [$-0.001297$, 0.002012]).} The result supports an outcome-specific additional-information claim, not a universal improvement claim.

\rev{The same-family nonlinear audit did not reproduce the Ridge pattern exactly (Appendix~B). Under HistGradientBoosting, adding profiles and temporal changes lowered exit-intensity MAE in the same direction as Ridge (grouped 0.005394 to 0.005309, $R^2$ 0.580 to 0.605; chronological 0.006209 to 0.006014), but the paired video-bootstrap intervals included zero (grouped improvement 0.000085, 95\% interval [$-0.000063$, 0.000236]; chronological 0.000195, [$-0.000248$, 0.000628]). Conversely, adding the profiles under HistGradientBoosting reduced grouped error for audience watch ratio (0.001396, [0.000286, 0.002533]) and relative retention (0.001358, [0.000087, 0.002685]), outcomes for which the Ridge changes had intervals that included zero. In a post hoc check added during the final revision and not pre-specified, weighting every window rather than every video equally kept the grouped Ridge exit-intensity and HistGradientBoosting audience-watch-ratio intervals above zero, but the HistGradientBoosting relative-retention interval included zero (0.001137, [$-0.000090$, 0.002455]; Appendix~B), so that gain depends on the weighting. Whether the cortical descriptors add information depends on both the outcome and the model family.}

\subsection{Cross-channel transfer is not established}
The auxiliary public-view stress test did not support direct cross-channel generalization. Its target was $\log(1+\text{public views})$, with log duration and log upload age as predictors; the enriched model added the six video-level cortical profiles. Each Ridge model and its preprocessing were fit on one channel and transferred unchanged to the other. Table~\ref{tab:transfer} reports the held-out $R^2$ values. These negative values indicate performance below the test-set mean baseline, not negative correlations. This boundary result means the primary-channel relationships should not be treated as a universal law for military video.

\rev{A diagnostic analysis of the per-video data describes conditions associated with the failure (Appendix~B); it does not establish the cause. First, the channels differ in target scale: mean $\log(1+\text{views})$ is 9.27 for the primary channel and 9.93 for KFN, and a constant predictor equal to the source-channel mean already scores $R^2=-0.17$ (primary $\rightarrow$ KFN) and $-0.34$ (KFN $\rightarrow$ primary). A mean shift of this size is thus arithmetically sufficient for negative scores of this magnitude; this does not show that it produced the scores of the fitted models. Second, the cortical profiles are the most shifted inputs: standardized mean differences reach $-1.13$ (visual), $-1.01$ (anterior-insular), and $+0.99$ (auditory), larger than the metadata shifts (duration $0.45$, upload age $0.05$). Such a shift would be expected if the channels differ in content mix, but content mix was not measured. Third, the same pipeline attains positive held-out $R^2$ within each channel (5-fold: $0.144\pm0.163$ primary, $0.239\pm0.030$ KFN), so it is not uninformative within either channel. Fourth, in the original run (\mbox{Table~\ref{tab:transfer}}), adding the profiles changed the two directions differently. From the primary channel to KFN, it improved MAE (1.295 to 1.249), $R^2$, and the rank correlation ($-0.345$ to $+0.074$). From KFN to the primary channel, it worsened MAE (1.280 to 1.514) and $R^2$ ($-0.755$ to $-1.348$) but raised the rank correlation ($-0.333$ to $-0.083$). The first three diagnostics were computed in this revision from the per-video data. The recomputed transfer models do not reproduce the original run exactly, and in them the primary $\rightarrow$ KFN change in MAE had the opposite direction (1.231 to 1.258). The direction of that MAE change therefore depends on the regularization strength (Appendix~B).}

\subsection{Pattern-change locations form a review queue}
The event analysis identified 93 candidate transitions across 85 videos; 92 matched event/control pairs across 84 videos had complete values for the primary display. For each transition, the plotted change is the value in the following five-second window minus the value in the preceding five-second window; the event-minus-control contrast subtracts the matched-control change from the event change. Audience watch ratio changed by -0.150 at event transitions and -0.069 at controls, for an event-minus-control contrast of -0.0811. Exit intensity changed by +0.00505 at events and -0.00207 at controls, for a contrast of +0.00713. Relative retention changed by -0.0215 at events and +0.0279 at controls, for a contrast of -0.0493. Figure~\ref{fig:events} presents the three outcome-specific contrasts with video-bootstrap intervals. The result identifies locations worth editorial inspection; it does not show that a cortical-pattern change caused an audience change.

\rev{This comparison shows that outcome changes around high-change transitions differed from those around matched control transitions, and that the 95\% video-bootstrap intervals of all three event-minus-control contrasts excluded zero. Controls were matched by nearest normalized position, but event and control transitions remained on average 0.19 of the video length apart, so position is not fully controlled. The comparison cannot estimate the effect of an edit: transitions also coincide with scene changes, text overlays, and other production events that the representation does not separate, and no intervention or randomization is involved. The display is therefore read as a descriptive co-variation screen for editorial inspection, not as evidence about what would change if the segment were edited.}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The findings differ by task. After position adjustment, cingulo-parietal and anterior-insular profiles were associated with aggregate retention\rev{, although the post hoc spatial-control comparison did not show these associations to exceed those of rotated cortical regions after correction}, while whole-cortex pattern change was associated with exit intensity. Adding profiles and temporal changes reduced exit-intensity error in video-grouped Ridge evaluation; the chronological estimate was similar but less certain, with its interval including zero. \rev{Under Ridge, the changes for the other two outcomes had intervals that included zero. Under HistGradientBoosting, the exit-intensity intervals included zero, audience-watch-ratio error decreased, and the relative-retention decrease depended on whether videos or windows were weighted equally, so the incremental value depends on the outcome, the model, and the weighting.} The event analysis provides time-local review candidates, not automated editing decisions.

\rev{The event-control contrast is a matched pre-post comparison. Matching by nearest normalized position reduces, but does not remove, position differences (mean distance 0.19 of the video length). It also does not control local trends or time-varying confounders that coincide with high-change transitions, such as scene cuts, captions, and sound events, which remain embedded in both series. The comparison therefore supports descriptive claims about co-variation magnitude, not causal identification, and no counterfactual editing effect is estimated \mbox{\cite{bertrand2004did,yarkoni2017prediction}}.}

The workflow supports complementary pre- and post-publication review. Before release, content descriptors can flag high-change segments without audience outcomes; after release, they can be aligned with aggregate platform reports to identify intervals for joint inspection. Preserving the profile, interval, and outcome keeps the basis of a review cue visible rather than hiding it in a composite score \cite{nardo2005composite}. Neither use prescribes edits, certifies educational quality, or evaluates service members.

\rev{Beyond this application, the analysis modules are generic: anatomically partitioned regional summaries, temporal pattern-change descriptors, and polynomial position residualization are standard pattern-analysis operations that could in principle transfer to other time-indexed signal domains, such as remote-sensing image time series. Such extensions lie outside the present evidence and would require their own validation.}

The cross-channel stress test did not support direct transfer: both channels used log public views, but channel context and video composition were not controlled, so the result cannot identify the source of the difference \cite{recht2019imagenet}. \rev{The diagnostics point to two candidate sources, a difference in target scale and a shift in the distribution of the cortical profiles, while the same pipeline retains positive held-out performance within each channel. Neither candidate was tested as a cause, so the diagnostics describe conditions associated with the failure and do not identify its cause. Because owner-authorized retention reports are available only for the channel operated by the authors' institution, an identically conditioned cross-channel retention study cannot be constructed from the present data, and this revision does not include one. The workflow's evidence is accordingly confined to the primary channel, and} this motivates prospective evaluation on a new, preregistered upload cohort.

Interpretation remains bounded: four videos had no retention rows; profiles summarize model predictions, not viewers' brain activity or psychological states; and aggregate retention does not reveal comprehension, learning, or individual departures. The retrospective analyses were not preregistered \cite{nosek2018preregistration}, and selected events may coincide with scene boundaries or production changes. \rev{The profiles also have anatomical limits. The cited functional peaks correspond only partly to the parcels, and the peak mapping carries registration error of several millimeters. The spatial-control comparison was added after the original results were known, its null is conditional on retained vertices, and its power is limited. The profiles therefore support a literature-grounded, anatomically defined summary of the model's content representation. They do not support a claim that a particular region carries a distinctive retention signal.}

\rev{Specifically, the cubic residualization order, the eight-valid-window eligibility rule, and the top-decile event rule were set in the analysis code, the window widths in the analysis configuration, and the 0.05 text-alignment threshold is the default of the inference pipeline. These were retrospective choices. In particular, the five-second primary window was not selected through a systematic comparison of candidate widths, and no record documents why the other values were chosen or shows that alternatives were compared. The window-width sensitivity analyses, the circular-shift nulls, and the released configuration bound, but do not eliminate, this analyst-degrees-of-freedom risk; the sensitivity analyses do not remove the uncertainty attached to the original window-width choice. In the same videos, the position-adjusted associations were about half as large with three-second windows as with five-second windows.} KFN results are a transfer stress test, not an independent validation cohort.

The next study should freeze outcomes, features, folds, windows, null procedure, and reporting before new uploads, then evaluate nominated segments with trained reviewers. Whether this process changes editing decisions or educational outcomes requires a controlled editing study \cite{shadish2002experimental}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study presents an auditable procedure for aligning TRIBE v2 content representations with owner-authorized audience-retention reports. In the primary channel, cortical profiles and whole-cortex pattern changes showed outcome-specific associations, and enriched features reduced grouped exit-intensity error\rev{ under Ridge, while the nonlinear audit showed outcome-dependent results}; cross-channel transfer was not supported. \rev{The present evidence for the workflow is therefore confined to the primary channel until prospective multi-channel validation.} The workflow offers time-local candidates for human editorial review, pending prospective evaluation.
